Corticosteroid + Amniotic-Fluid Exosome / Secretome Combination in Chronic Subdural Hematoma
A Two-Signal Convergence Hypothesis: Inflammatory Suppression Coupled to Active Resolution
Investigator & Clinician Briefing — Working Scientific Hypothesis
Document  ·  Version 1.0
Prepared for: Atunse Healthcare Clinical Team
1.  Executive summary
Chronic subdural hematoma (CSDH) is no longer understood as a passive collection of degraded blood. Contemporary reviews describe it as a biologically active, self-perpetuating disorder of the subdural membrane, driven by the interaction of inflammation, fragile neovascularization, vascular leak, fibrinolytic dysregulation and matrix remodeling.
A development in the recent literature reframes the therapeutic problem. Recurrence is increasingly attributed to immune dysregulation — specifically an imbalance in macrophage polarization (a skew toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype) — rather than to the magnitude of inflammation alone. This distinction explains a persistent clinical paradox: corticosteroid monotherapy reliably suppresses inflammatory signaling yet has produced inconsistent, and in the largest randomized trial unfavorable, clinical outcomes.
Because symptom relief in CSDH can precede measurable change in hematoma volume, this model deliberately separates two endpoint tiers: an early, symptom-level signal (reduced meningeal irritation and pressure sensitivity) and a delayed, structural endpoint (hematoma volume and recurrence), which is confirmatory and must be established by imaging. A defined safety rail governs the one mechanistic axis — angiogenesis — where the AF-EV contribution could plausibly act in either a favorable or an unfavorable direction.
2.  CSDH is a biologically active target
Reviews and consensus statements describe CSDH as a self-perpetuating cycle that includes inflammation, fragile neovascularization, vascular leakage and transudation, fibrinolytic dysregulation, recurrent microhemorrhage, and structural membrane remodeling [1–4]. The outer neomembrane becomes highly vascularized with fragile, leaky capillaries supported by VEGF, matrix metalloproteinases, and angiopoietin-2 / Tie2 signaling, which perpetuates re-bleeding and fluid accumulation [1, 9].
This framework matters clinically because headache and pressure symptoms can improve before the hematoma disappears. Rapid symptom relief may reflect reduced meningeal inflammatory signaling, reduced vascular permeability or edema, improved neurovascular pressure tolerance, or reduced pain sensitization. Objective hematoma resolution is a separate, slower process that still requires serial imaging to demonstrate.
3.  The corticosteroid arm: reducing inflammatory gain and vascular permeability
Corticosteroids act through the intracellular glucocorticoid receptor (NR3C1). Once ligand-bound, the receptor can transrepress pro-inflammatory transcription factors such as NF-κB and AP-1, reducing the expression of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, adhesion molecules, prostaglandin pathway components, and matrix-remodeling mediators [8]. Endothelial cells are themselves glucocorticoid-responsive; glucocorticoids regulate adhesion molecule expression, inflammatory cytokine and chemokine output, and barrier integrity [9].
In CSDH the steroid-relevant targets plausibly include IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8/CXCL8, COX-2/PGE2, iNOS/NO, VEGF-associated permeability, MMP-2/MMP-9 remodeling, endothelial activation, and membrane inflammation. Reducing this signaling may lower local irritation and edema before any measurable hematoma shrinkage occurs.
3.1  The clinical paradox of steroid monotherapy
The evidence for corticosteroids as a stand-alone therapy is genuinely mixed, and this is a feature of the rationale rather than an inconvenience to be minimized. In the randomized Dex-CSDH trial, dexamethasone produced fewer favorable outcomes and more adverse events than placebo at six months, even though it reduced the rate of repeat surgery for recurrence [12]. A separate randomized program comparing dexamethasone with surgery was discontinued early over safety and non-inferiority concerns [13]. Conversely, smaller and retrospective series — particularly of low-dose, adjuvant, time-limited regimens in selected high-risk patients — have reported reduced recurrence with acceptable tolerability [referenced in 12, 13].
The reconciling interpretation is mechanistic: corticosteroids deliver a real but partial signal. They damp the inflammatory amplitude but do not instruct the macrophage compartment to resolve, and at the systemic doses required for monotherapy they carry dose-related harm in an elderly, comorbid population. A combination that supplies the resolution signal separately could, in principle, decouple benefit from steroid dose — the core translational promise of this hypothesis.
4.  The AF exosome / secretome arm: a resolution-biased signal
Amniotic fluid contains extracellular vesicles (EVs) and soluble proteins and growth factors originating from the fetoplacental unit; AF-derived EVs are described as biological messengers carrying active molecular cargo within a lipid bilayer [5]. Perinatal-cell-derived EVs have been reviewed as anti-inflammatory and regenerative signaling products capable of modulating inflammatory microenvironments [6].
A human amniotic-fluid EV study reported suppressive effects on T-cell proliferation, T-cell activation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release in vitro [7]. More directly relevant to the present model, a 2025 study of human amniotic-fluid stem-cell EVs reported macrophage immune modulation and identified the surface marker CD44 as a delivery code that directs preferential uptake of EVs by inflammatory macrophages; blocking CD44 abolished both uptake and the downstream suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [10-AF, here cited as reference 10b]. This provides a candidate mechanism for selectivity — the resolution signal is preferentially taken up by the very cells that are driving the pathology.
4.1  Convergence on the macrophage switch
The mechanistic centerpiece of this hypothesis is macrophage repolarization. Across multiple tissue systems, MSC- and perinatal-cell-derived EVs have been shown to switch macrophages from an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype toward an M2-like reparative phenotype, downregulating IL-6 and iNOS/NOS2 and upregulating Arg1, CD206 and CD163, with associated suppression of JAK/STAT1 signaling [6, 14, 15, 16]. CD44- and decorin-dependent routes to M2 polarization have been described as master immunoregulatory mechanisms in perinatal and umbilical-cord systems [10b, 17].
Placed against the CSDH finding that an M1-skewed polarization state drives recurrence [10], the convergence is direct: the pathology is defined by a polarization imbalance, and the AF-EV signal is hypothesized to correct that exact imbalance. This is the node at which the two arms of the combination meet and where their effects are expected to be more than additive.
4.2  Candidate cellular and molecular targets
Cellular compartments: monocytes/macrophages (primary), T cells, endothelial cells, meningeal cells, and perivascular immune compartments.
Candidate effects: reduced inflammatory cytokine production; macrophage transition toward a resolution/repair phenotype; endothelial barrier support; anti-apoptotic tissue signaling; and normalization rather than amplification of extracellular-matrix remodeling.
5.  The two-signal convergence hypothesis
Hypothesis statement. In chronic subdural hematoma, corticosteroids reduce the inflammatory amplification state of the membrane (suppression), while AF exosome / secretome cargo supplies a resolution-biased, macrophage-directed immunomodulatory and endothelial-support signal that corrects the M1-skewed polarization imbalance (resolution). Acting together, the two signals are hypothesized to lower meningeal irritation and pressure sensitivity faster than either input alone, and — over a longer horizon and subject to imaging confirmation — to bias the membrane toward organized resorption and reduced recurrence, potentially at lower corticosteroid exposure than monotherapy would require.

8.  Biomarkers and translational endpoints
The endpoint architecture is deliberately tiered. The first tier tests whether symptom change tracks reduced inflammatory tone and a corrected polarization state; the second, confirmatory tier tests whether the membrane is structurally resolving. The macrophage/resolution panel is elevated relative to the original draft because it is the direct readout of the pivotal convergence node.
9.  Open questions and next steps
Cargo characterization: does the AF-EV product, by lot, carry a net resolution-biased or net pro-angiogenic cargo profile? This is the gating question for the safety rail.
Sequencing and timing: are the two signals best delivered concurrently, or does suppression-then-resolution (or the reverse) better match the temporal biology of the membrane cycle?
Dose-sparing hypothesis: can the addition of the resolution signal achieve the symptom and (imaging-confirmed) structural benefit at a corticosteroid dose low enough to avoid the harm seen in Dex-CSDH?
Route of administration and access to the subdural compartment: what delivery route gives the AF-EV signal access to the relevant macrophage compartment?
Patient selection: do CT subtype and baseline M1/M2 / biomarker profile identify the patients most likely to benefit, consistent with the polarization-by-subtype findings [10]?

Status and intended use This document presents a hypothesis-stage mechanistic model. It is intended to frame scientific reasoning and to guide biomarker selection, study design, and safety monitoring for investigators and clinicians. It is not a treatment protocol, not a marketing claim, and not a statement of proven efficacy. No claim of hematoma resolution may be made on the basis of this model; structural change in chronic subdural hematoma must be confirmed by serial neuroimaging.

--- TABLE 2 ---
Central thesis Corticosteroids are a suppression signal: they lower the inflammatory gain of the CSDH membrane. They do not, by themselves, supply a resolution signal — an instruction that actively reprograms the immune microenvironment toward repair. Amniotic-fluid (AF) exosome / secretome cargo is hypothesized to supply precisely that missing resolution signal, biasing macrophages from an M1-like toward an M2-like reparative phenotype. The combination is therefore not two anti-inflammatories stacked together; it is the pairing of suppression with active resolution. If the model holds, adding a resolution signal could allow clinical benefit at lower corticosteroid exposure, mitigating the dose-related harm seen with steroid monotherapy.

--- TABLE 3 ---
Why this reframing changes the therapeutic logic A 2025 analysis of macrophage polarization across CSDH subtypes reported that the M1/M2 ratio shifts toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype in evolving and recurrence-prone hematomas, with corresponding rises in M1-associated cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α) and that immune dysregulation — not inflammation in isolation — is the more accurate driver of persistence and recurrence [10]. The authors note explicitly that the efficacy of anti-inflammatory drug therapy alone is limited. This is the single most important rationale for the present combination: a therapy that only suppresses inflammation may not correct the underlying polarization imbalance, whereas a resolution-biased signal directly targets it.

--- TABLE 4 ---
Evidentiary boundary None of the AF-EV studies cited above were performed in chronic subdural hematoma. They establish biological plausibility for a resolution-biased, macrophage-directed mechanism; they do not establish benefit in CSDH. Every forward-looking statement in this document should be read with that boundary in mind, and the program is structured to test — not assume — the model.
